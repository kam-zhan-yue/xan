## Banks

Why is it so hard to open a bank account in Japan. For the first six months you enter as a foreigner, you can only open
one type of bank account - the Japan Post (ゆうちょ) Cash Card. This is not a credit nor debit card, but simply an account
that you can store cash in and withdraw from. You are better off keeping your money under your bed as there are fees for 
every withdrawal. Of course, there is the benefit of receiving money from domestic accounts. But there are additional rules
for sending money. For the first six months, any outgoing transfer (domestic or international) incurs an additional ¥7,000 fee.

For every transfer.

After my first six months (which resets every time you come back to the country under a new residential status, by the way),
I was motivated to open a proper bank account. I read that banks are starting to have fully online processes, so I tried that.

- I first tried to open an account with Mitsui Sumitomo (三井住友) and their Olive card. After completing their online
procedure (which took an hour), I received an SMS the next day. The documents you provided are insufficient, please schedule an
in-person appointment. Now, it's not that they were insufficient, it was because I was a foreign national. And I couldn't schedule
an in-person appointment because I was undergoing the mandatory company 研修.
- My colleagues recommended I check out Minna no Ginko (みんなの銀行), an online-first bank where you can conduct everything
through their app, similar to Australia's Up bank. I heard good things about Up bank and was excited to learn that Japan was 
starting to make something similar. But alas, I have learned to never get my hopes up. There's always a catch. And in this case,
the catch was that you are not allowed to open an account with them unless you're a Japanese citizen.

## Online Forms and My Pages

Every service in Japan has their own website. Every service in Japan has their own My Page (an equivalent to your dashboard).
Every website has their own password restrictions. Every website has their own system of ID.

- Some websites use your email as your ID
- Some websites use a custom username as your ID
- Some websites use an unreadable UID as your ID

And what happens if you forget your ID? You can't just put in your email, no no. Sometimes you need your email, your phone 
number, your date of birth, and your name. And you have to input your name in katakana. Now this sucks for me because my
name is not easily inputted in katakana. My full name is Kam Zhan Yue. In katakana, this is カム・ザン・ユー. But sometimes,
websites don't let me put that interval dot between the Zhan and Yue. So sometimes it is ザン・ユー or ザンユー. You see 
the problem? I hate it here.

And once you go through the entire process of registering, a process that is meant to make your life easier and reduce
the friction you go through with in-person processes, sometimes you have to wait DAYS for a response because a human has
to check your registration. What's the point. What was the point? This country is hard to live in.

And why did you forget your ID? Because it was in an email sent to you months ago. An email that is literally impossible to find
because you have 100 other emails just like that one.
